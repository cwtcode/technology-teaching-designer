# Required framework alignment for AI lessons

## Scope and source control

Apply this requirement to lessons and curriculum units about AI, AI literacy, substantive learning through AI-assisted application development, vibe coding and agentic development. It does not apply merely because a teacher used AI to prepare a non-AI lesson. Retain local curriculum, school policy, age/access requirements and subject objectives.

Use both student-facing reference frameworks below. Verify any revised edition before adoption; do not silently mix editions or refer to a draft as final. Record the verification date, source title, issuer and locator in the plan or source notes. Sources checked on 26 October 2026:

Retrieve official documents or read a supplied matching edition before claiming precise descriptor alignment. If access is unavailable, use the verified structural synopsis below and explicitly label descriptor-level mappings provisional. Never invent competence numbers, page references, quotations or certification claims. Distinguish printed page numbers from PDF viewer indices.

## OECD/EU student AI literacy structure

Use the final 2026 domain names:
- **Engage with AI:** recognise AI, examine its outputs and implications.
- **Create with AI:** develop ideas with AI while retaining human direction.
- **Manage AI:** decide whether and how work is delegated and supervised.
- **Shape AI:** investigate and improve AI systems for human benefit.

Map the selected competence to the relevant knowledge, skills and attitudes and an appropriate **Basic, Intermediate or Advanced** learner expectation. These levels are not age/grade bands and are not UNESCO’s levels. Foundational engagement supports later learning; creating and managing can develop alongside one another. Do not invent a rigid year-by-year ladder.

Verified locators in the 2026 PDF: domain overview printed p. 8; expectations/progression p. 25; Engage pp. 26–31; Create pp. 32–35; Manage pp. 36–39; Shape pp. 40–43. Example competence labels verified in the publication include Engage with AI 3 (evaluating output) and Shape AI 2 (evaluation using criteria and tests). Retrieve the descriptor and chosen learner expectation before using an exact mapping. Use domain-level wording if the competence cannot be checked.

## UNESCO student structure and progression

Keep all four aspects visible across a curriculum, while selecting relevant competencies for an individual lesson. The matrix below preserves competency labels from Table 1; consult full specifications before judging mastery.

| Aspect | Understand | Apply | Create |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human-centred mindset | Human agency | Human accountability | Citizenship in the era of AI |
| Ethics of AI | Embodied ethics | Safe and responsible use | Ethics by design |
| AI techniques and applications | AI foundations | Application skills | Creating AI tools |
| AI system design | Problem scoping | Architecture design | Iteration and feedback loops |

Do not assume that producing an artifact reaches Create, or that executing a prompt demonstrates system design. Explain what learner reasoning and technical work support the selected competency. Treat progression as adaptable and evidence-informed, not a fixed age assignment.

For teacher preparation, the separate teacher framework covers human-centred mindset, ethics, AI foundations/applications, AI pedagogy and professional learning, with Acquire/Deepen/Create levels. Use it for teacher readiness or professional development; keep those levels out of the student crosswalk.

## Required lesson-design procedure

1. Identify students’ existing AI understanding and an appropriate conceptual entry point; distinguish AI from ordinary rule-based software where relevant.
2. Select a manageable number of relevant competencies from each student framework. Confirm precise descriptors and readiness levels. Do not demand all domains, aspects or levels in one lesson.
3. Write observable objectives and success criteria first. State what students will explain, evaluate, decide, test or revise.
4. Design at least one bounded task completed and assessed within the lesson, with platform, time allocation, minimum output, scaffolds and fallback.
5. Map every AI objective to relevant competencies from both frameworks, or explain a justified partial/domain-level mapping. Do not manufacture one-to-one equivalence between frameworks. If a proposed topic has no meaningful AI-learning outcome, treat it as a non-AI subject lesson rather than attaching decorative labels.
6. Include a human decision and appropriate verification in AI-use tasks. Collect individual evidence even when the product is made by a pair or generated with AI.
7. Review coverage across the sequence: conceptual/technical understanding, human agency, critical judgement, responsible use, and impacts should develop coherently. Select relevant issues such as bias, privacy, attribution, inclusion or sustainability rather than adding every issue to every lesson.
8. Preserve the school format: Time | Content | Activity | Remarks. Put the compact crosswalk in Teacher notes and use Remarks for objective/task links. Keep student language concrete.

## Application to vibe coding and agentic development

Require learners to formulate the problem, explain what they delegate, inspect outputs, test independently, act on feedback, and justify acceptance/rejection. Assess the final product and the human reasoning separately. Use bounded tools, synthetic data, reversible changes and explicit human review points where needed.

An ordinary app generated with AI may demonstrate AI-assisted creation, management or evaluation. It does not automatically demonstrate building/improving an AI system. Claim system-design alignment only when learners actually investigate or alter an AI system’s components, data, behaviour, architecture or evaluation as required by the selected descriptor.

Unplugged cases and teacher-prepared AI examples can support conceptual/evaluation objectives when live tools are unsuitable. Do not claim that paper evaluation alone proves independent operation of an AI tool. Do not require public accounts, publishing or agent deployment solely to obtain assessment evidence.

## Small illustrative planning pattern

For an introductory output-evaluation lesson, learners inspect a teacher-prepared AI response, check it against supplied evidence and decide what to accept, revise or reject. Finish with a corrected response and an individual explanation of the verification and human decision. Consider OECD/EU Engage with AI and UNESCO human-centred/ethical or application competencies, checking specific descriptors and readiness before finalising the crosswalk. This is a teacher-designed pattern, not an official mandated lesson or an assertion that every such task meets Apply/Create.
