# Learning rationale, inquiry and agency

## Select, explain, evaluate

Select one or two relevant perspectives for a particular lesson. Connect design choice -> proposed learning mechanism -> observable evidence -> possible adjustment. Use cautious language: a rationale supports a design; actual evidence informs effectiveness. Do not invent research, citations, student outcomes, or learning gains. Browse/verify sources when citing research. Do not diagnose fixed learning styles or assign teaching to unsupported visual/auditory learner categories.

| Perspective | Design use | Evidence and possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Constructivism | Elicit prior ideas, investigate a problem, revise explanations | Compare initial and revised reasoning; address persistent misconceptions |
| Social constructivism and scaffolding | Model reasoning, use purposeful dialogue, fade temporary support | Check individual transfer with less assistance; restore targeted support if needed |
| Cognitive load theory | Segment complex procedures, integrate related explanations, use worked examples | Observe loss of place and transfer performance; simplify presentation or adjust guidance |
| Self-regulated learning | Plan, monitor, select strategies, reflect | Examine decisions and help-seeking; model diagnosis if students cannot choose next steps |
| Self-determination theory | Support meaningful autonomy, achievable competence, respectful relationships | Use student feedback plus participation evidence; adjust choice, challenge, or group structure |

Treat Bloom's taxonomy as a cognitive-demand framework, not a learning theory or a mandatory staircase. Treat formative assessment as an assessment approach. Treat inquiry and project-based learning as approaches; distinguish a full project from a single practical activity. Avoid inferring motivation from on-task behaviour alone.

## Inquiry levels

- Structured: provide question and procedure; students predict, interpret evidence, and justify conclusions.
- Guided: provide problem, goals, constraints; students choose questions, methods, solution design, or tests.
- Open: provide broad theme and criteria; students define an investigable problem and method, subject to readiness and feasibility.

Start from students' experience and task complexity. Inquiry can coexist with explicit instruction. Limit scope before removing support. Do not require open inquiry for every lesson.

## Reusable inquiry methods

- Predict-observe-explain: collect a prediction and reason, run the example, reconcile the result.
- Debugging investigation: form competing hypotheses, choose discriminating tests, explain the fix.
- Design inquiry: investigate needs, prototype, gather user evidence, revise against criteria.
- Comparative investigation: evaluate alternatives with agreed measures and acknowledge trade-offs.
- Student-generated questions: collect questions, distinguish testable from overly broad ones, agree a feasible investigation.

## Agency specification

For each major inquiry activity identify:
Question | teacher constraints | student decisions | available scaffolds | evidence | next action.

Support students to set a goal, choose a strategy, request help, challenge a suggestion, and revise a decision. Include an explanation of why a choice matters. Theme or colour choice alone is insufficient when claiming agency in reasoning. Co-construct criteria where appropriate while preserving essential curriculum outcomes.

For group work, rotate roles and require individual explanation, prediction, or adaptation. For AI use, ask students to propose an initial plan, document the help requested, evaluate suggestions, and explain accepted/rejected changes. Avoid collecting complete private chat logs unnecessarily.

## Feedback and post-lesson review

Use prompts such as "The test showed...", "This meets criterion... because...", "A useful change would be...", and "I changed... after...". Reserve revision time.

Separate observation, interpretation, theoretical explanation, and next action in teacher reflection. Ask whether students could explain, adapt, test, and transfer learning, not simply finish. Use provided work samples, exit responses, error patterns, and student voice. Offer alternative explanations and a specific next check. Label all example evidence as hypothetical.
