## Select delivery mode

**Embedded:** add one or two relevant literacy outcomes to a host lesson. Identify the exact activity, allocated minutes, source/platform, expected output, criteria and evidence. Integrate it into the existing task and feedback rather than appending generic warnings. Reduce other scope if needed. Preserve the subject learning and assess the literacy element explicitly.

Examples: check factual sources before writing a quiz; examine a chart’s scale in a mathematics data lesson; compare source claims before creating a science explainer; document attribution and privacy choices in an app project. Do not require an unrelated source-evaluation exercise in every basic logic lesson.

**Standalone:** make literacy the main learning purpose. Use an inquiry question such as “What evidence would justify sharing this claim?” Follow instruction -> source-analysis demonstration -> guided investigation -> peer/teacher feedback and revision -> individual conclusion. Complete at least one assessed evaluation or communication task during the lesson. A discussion alone needs an observable individual response.

**Across a curriculum:** combine explicit introductory lessons with repeated application across subjects. Identify where each selected target is introduced, practised, assessed and revisited. Allocate responsibilities and time without claiming that all nine areas must be taught in every lesson.

## Match the stage and readiness

Hong Kong stages in the source: KS1 P1–P3; KS2 P4–P6; KS3 S1–S3; KS4 S4–S6. S2 therefore uses KS3 targets, with support adapted to actual experience. Do not mechanically equate these bands with other jurisdictions.

- Early primary: guided identification of sources, simple perspectives, false information, respectful sharing and personal-information protection.
- Upper primary: simple search terms, fact/opinion distinctions, basic checking criteria, safe sharing and attribution.
- Junior secondary: investigate context, source purpose and credibility; fact-check before sharing; examine advertisements, viewpoints, representations and effects. The Area 4 KS3 targets are on p. 10; search strategy targets are on p. 9; synthesis/content creation on p. 11; provider/representation targets on p. 12.
- Senior secondary: deepen contextual interpretation, evidence synthesis, contradictions, commercial influence and the responsibilities involved in sharing.

Use pp. 8–13 for precise target selection. For Area 9, teach the relevant basic technology concepts before discussing its ethical implications, as the note on p. 13 advises. This does not make coding a prerequisite for all literacy learning.

## Practical inquiry and assessment workflow

1. Identify the information need, claim or message; distinguish checkable claims from opinions and persuasion. Mixed messages can contain all three.
2. Ask who produced it, when, for whom, for what purpose, and in what context. Inspect evidence, not appearance or popularity alone.
3. Trace an important claim to its original evidence. Compare genuinely independent sources where feasible; copied versions do not constitute independent confirmation.
4. Examine missing context, date, editing/cropping, scale, sponsor incentives, representation and uncertainty as relevant. A viewpoint or bias does not automatically make every factual claim false.
5. Reach a proportionate judgement: supported, contradicted, misleading through context, or not yet verifiable. Explain the evidence and limits. Do not infer deliberate disinformation solely from an incorrect claim.
6. Decide whether/how to share, revise, attribute, seek more evidence, or withhold. Consider audience, privacy, rights and possible effects.
7. Act on feedback and explain the revision individually.

Do not treat an AI detector, visual impression, high ranking, polished site or number of reposts as definitive verification. AI is optional: design equally valid tasks using human-authored sources, advertisements, charts, videos or printed packs.

## Completed tasks and platforms

Recommend a platform suited to the actual activity: a school-approved browser and library/search resources for investigation; an existing document tool for an evidence table; an annotation/presentation tool for media analysis; or a prepared paper pack when devices/connectivity are limited. Name the chosen tool in an actual plan and check current access requirements rather than imposing accounts. Do not require a coding platform for a literacy lesson. Public posting is not necessary for assessment.

Illustrative standalone 40-minute S2 task: provide a short fictional social post and two dated source extracts with enough relevant evidence. Students complete a one-page check with the claim, source/context analysis, evidence comparison, uncertainty and justified sharing decision. Budget 5 minutes for the question/goals, 7 for a worked example, 15 for investigation, 8 for feedback/revision, and 5 for an individual conclusion. Adjust to the reading demand. Label invented cases as fictional; do not describe supplied extracts as independent unless checked.

Minimum criteria: identify the claim and provenance; use relevant evidence and evaluate source independence/context; justify a judgement including uncertainty; make a responsible communication decision. Pair investigation must include an individual explanation. Supported route: shorter annotated sources and prompts; extension: investigate a missing source or revise a misleading headline. Use an evidence record with source/date/context, claim, supporting/contradicting evidence, limitations and decision.

Illustrative embedded task: before publishing an app’s quiz content, each pair verifies one claim, identifies the source/date, records an asset’s permitted use and revises any unsupported wording. Each learner explains one evidence or rights decision. Assess the content decision alongside the functional task; allocate explicit lesson time and avoid overloading the objectives.

## Anticipate misconceptions

- “First search result means true”: compare ranking with evidence and source purpose.
- “Many reposts prove it”: trace whether the reposts share one origin.
- “Opinion means false”: separate a factual claim from evaluative language.
- “Old means false” or “new means accurate”: assess relevance, context and changes.
- “A photo proves the whole story”: inspect provenance, date, caption and cropping.
- “Online means free to reuse”: check the actual licence/permission and attribution.
- “A working app or polished presentation proves accuracy”: assess its underlying claims separately.
- “Uncertain means wrong”: require a justified limit and a sensible next verification step.

Use age-appropriate neutral or clearly fictional cases. Do not ask students to expose private accounts, repeat harmful rumours about classmates, generate deceptive content targeting real people, or post unverified material publicly. Assess evidence-based reasoning rather than agreement with a predetermined viewpoint.
